Case study · Bowker Transport · Contract, 2024–2026

Bowker Transport: one dispatch platform, nothing typed twice.

An internal platform that replaced the spreadsheets and phone calls dispatch ran on. I was the Product Owner on contract for 15 months, from on-site discovery to the spreadsheet switch-off and the handover to an internal owner, with design oversight of the interface.

Role
Product Owner on contract for the dispatch platform and its rollout, with design oversight of the planning board and the driver web app
Team
5 engineers, 1 data engineer, 1 QA and a part-time UI designer, plus a power user in each warehouse
Duration
15 months: 12 initial, extended by 3
Outcome
Planning admin per dispatcher down from 3.5 to 1.6 hours a day, and 96% of shipments planned in the platform when spreadsheets were switched off.
01

Context

Bowker Transport stores goods for manufacturers and retailers and delivers them. The work that holds it together is dispatch.

Every afternoon dispatchers take the next day's orders, group them into loads and assign trucks and drivers. The next day they follow the deliveries and deal with whatever goes wrong.

Dispatch ran on three systems that didn't talk to each other: a warehouse system (WMS) for stock and picking, a transport system (TMS) for transport orders and the ERP for billing, held together by spreadsheets and phone calls. A route-planning tool bought two years earlier had been abandoned within months, so the project also had to win back the dispatchers' trust.

02

Problem

Dispatchers retyped every order between three systems and tracked trucks by phone. Nobody had a live view of the day, and errors surfaced only when a customer complained.

  • A mistyped address or delivery window became a late delivery.
  • Nobody could see where the trucks were without calling drivers.
  • Paper delivery notes took days to reach the office, which delayed invoices and fed disputes.
  • Customers were asking for digital proof of delivery and same-day answers on delivery status.

A time study in the first month put a number on it: 3.5 hours a day per dispatcher went on collecting orders, retyping and phone calls. That became the baseline.

Constraints

  • A legacy system

    A 15-year-old WMS with no APIs.

  • Peak season

    No go-lives from mid-November to the end of December.

  • Trust

    Dispatchers were sceptical after the last tool failed.

03

My role and what I owned

I owned the product: its goal, its backlog and the order of the backlog. The squad built it, operations decided when a site was ready, and I answered for the outcome.

  • Product Goal, OKRs and the outcome roadmap, agreed with the operations director as sponsor.
  • The Product Backlog: refinement, ordering and acceptance against the Definition of Done, with MoSCoW priorities for each release.
  • Sprint reviews with the people who use the platform. What the squad learned there fed back into the order of the backlog.
  • Discovery: shadowing dispatchers, warehouse leads and drivers on day and night shifts at three sites, and the time study that set the baseline.
  • Go-live criteria per site, agreed with each site operations manager.
  • Measurement: weekly adoption per site, and the time study repeated in month 14.

Design oversight came with the role. After the shadowing I wrote four principles the squad worked to: one screen for the whole day, nothing typed twice, exceptions come to you, and keyboard before mouse. A part-time UI designer owned the final screens. I set the direction, tested it with users and reviewed every flow before backlog refinement.

The roadmap is written as outcomes, with the features underneath, and certainty drops from left to right. At month 8 it showed that the rollout could not finish inside the 12 months of the contract. That made the case for the three-month extension.

Releases were sliced along the dispatcher's day. Release 1 left the end of the day on paper so that the pilot could start sooner; proof of delivery followed in release 2.

Sample backlog item

See late shipments without calling drivers

As a dispatcher, I want an alert when a truck is likely to miss its delivery window, so that I can warn the customer or reassign the load before it's late.

Acceptance criteria

  • Given telematics shows a truck more than 20 minutes behind plan, then an alert appears in my exceptions list.
  • Given I open an alert, then I see the truck's position, the new arrival time and the customer contact.
  • Given a subcontracted truck without telematics, then its position comes from the driver web app or is shown as unknown, never guessed.
  • Alerts can be acknowledged and are logged for the weekly review.

Must for release 2 · 8 points · Sprint 17 · Serves the on-time delivery key result

05

Key decisions and trade-offs

Decision 1Integrate the old systems, don't replace them

The scope was integration first: orders arrive by themselves and plans are written back. The platform reads orders from the WMS, writes plans to the TMS and confirms deliveries to the ERP. The old systems stayed.

Chosen

Read the WMS export files every 15 minutes. That removed retyping within the project.

Rejected

Replacing the WMS. It would have taken two years.

Trade-off

Orders can appear up to 15 minutes late, and stock counts still happen on WMS screens.

Decision 2The dispatcher plans; the platform doesn't plan for them

Discovery showed why the last tool had failed: it produced automatic routes that dispatchers didn't trust and couldn't change, so they went back to spreadsheets. We tested the planning board with 9 dispatchers in three prototype rounds, first on paper. Round one killed the map-first layout I had expected to work: dispatchers plan from a list sorted by delivery window and use the map only to check.

Chosen

A planning board where the dispatcher drags orders onto loads and sees capacity as they go.

Rejected

Route optimisation in this phase. It went to the next-phase backlog as suggestions the dispatcher can accept or change. Trust comes first.

Trade-off

Building loads is no faster than before: one hour a day, unchanged.

Decision 3Musts under 60% of every release

Each release was prioritised with MoSCoW, together with the operations managers and the power users. I held the Musts under 60% of the planned points, so that a slip costs a Could, not a Must. Release 2 was planned at 102 points.

  • Must · 60 pts

    The release fails without it.

    • Stop list on the driver's phone · 13
    • Proof of delivery by phone · 13
    • Delivery confirmed to the ERP · 8
    • Late-truck alert · 8
    • Position from the driver's phone · 8
    • Missing-documents alert · 5
    • "Problem at the door" report · 5
  • Should · 21 pts

    Important, with a workaround.

    • Account manager status view · 8
    • Proof of delivery offline · 8
    • Flags for missing order data · 5
  • Could · 21 pts

    Only if time allows.

    • Split and merge loads · 8
    • Tracking link for customers · 13
  • Won't this release

    Agreed out, so nobody waits.

    • Route suggestions: next phase
    • Invoice sent on delivery: needs finance rules first
    • Native driver app: dropped for a web page

The tracking link for customers did not fit and moved to the next phase; customers got delivery status through their account manager instead. The native driver app was dropped for a web page that opens from a text message, because subcontracted drivers wouldn't install a company app. I rode along on six delivery routes: drivers wear gloves and have half a minute at the door, so proof of delivery became three taps: photo, name, done.

Decision 4One fixed date to switch off spreadsheets

Running both in parallel let people drift back to spreadsheets. The operations director set one date for all sites and held to it. Every warehouse followed the same eight-week playbook, with a power user trained first and two of us on site for the first two weeks, and went live only when its criteria were met.

Chosen

A single switch-off date, backed by go-live criteria for each site.

Rejected

Keeping spreadsheets running in parallel as a safety net.

Trade-off

Two weeks of extra on-site support around the date.

  • Check 1

    Before go-live

    • Order sync errors under 1% for a week
    • Every dispatcher trained on their own orders
    • A dry run on yesterday's orders matched the real plan
  • Check 2

    Sign-off at week +4

    • 90% of shipments in the platform, two weeks running
    • No open defects that block planning
    • The site operations manager signs off
06

Outcome

  • 3.5 h → 1.6 h

    planning admin per dispatcher per day, a 54% cut. Measured by time studies in month 1 and month 14.

  • 96%

    of shipments planned in the platform when spreadsheets were switched off. The target was 95%.

  • 84% → 89%

    on-time delivery across months 13 to 15, one point short of the 90% target. A shared result: operations also replaced a subcontractor that year.

The saving came from everything around the planning itself. Retyping went from 1.2 to 0.1 hours a day, collecting orders from 0.7 to 0.1 and calling drivers from 0.6 to 0.4. Building loads stayed at one hour, by design: the dispatcher still does the planning.

Orders retyped by hand went from all of them to 4%: the ones two customers still send by email, typed in once.

Dispatchers scored the old set of tools 48 out of 100 on the System Usability Scale; the platform scored 76 in month 14. Delivery notes now reach the office in minutes instead of days. At close-out, proof-of-delivery disputes were at about half the earlier level at the first three sites; it was too early to tell at the others.

Close-out. Five weeks of hypercare followed the switch-off. Ownership then moved to an internal product owner from operations, who had worked alongside me from month 12.

07

What I'd do next

Test the riskiest integration with real data in the first month, before agreeing a pilot date.

I underestimated the old WMS, and the pilot went live six weeks late. The next-phase backlog I handed over:

  • Route suggestions the dispatcher can accept or change.
  • Tracking links for customers.
  • Invoicing on delivery, once finance has agreed the rules.
